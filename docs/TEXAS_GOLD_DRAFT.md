## The page, as a reader would see it

**Texas Senate Bill 4 · 89th Legislature, Regular Session**
*Official:* Relating to an increase in the amount of the exemption of residence homesteads from ad valorem taxation
by a school district and the protection of school districts against certain losses in local revenue.

### Raises the part of a home's value that school taxes skip from $100,000 to $140,000. *(Analysis)*

**In plain English** *(Analysis)*
Texas homeowners pay school property taxes on their home's value minus a set amount. This bill raises that amount
from $100,000 to $140,000 for a home the owner lives in, and the state pays school districts for the money they
lose. It takes effect only if voters approve a matching change to the Texas Constitution; the Governor signed it on
June 16, 2025.

**What would change** *(Analysis)*

1. **The part of a home's value that school taxes do not count**
   Today: School districts do not tax the first $100,000 of the value of a home its owner lives in.
   If this passes: School districts would not tax the first $140,000, starting with the 2025 tax year. *(Sec. 1.01)*
2. **Money for school districts that collect less**
   Today: The state gives school districts extra money to make up for the 2023 increase in this tax break.
   If this passes: Beginning with the 2025-2026 school year, the state must also make up what districts lose from
   this increase. *(Sec. 1.03)*
3. **When the change starts**
   Today: The $100,000 amount stays in place unless voters approve a change to the Texas Constitution.
   If this passes: The $140,000 amount takes effect on the day the voters' approval takes effect. If voters say no,
   it never takes effect. *(Sec. 3.02)*
4. **What the state would pay**
   Today: The state pays nothing for this increase, because it is not in effect.
   If this passes: The state would pay about $1,324.2 million in the 2026 budget year and $1,418.4 million in 2027,
   the Legislative Budget Board estimates. *(Fiscal note)*

**How it works today** *(Analysis)*

- School districts in Texas tax homes based on their value. A home the owner lives in gets a set amount taken off
  its value before the school tax is figured. *(Sec. 1.01)*
- The amount was $15,000 until 2015, when voters approved $25,000. Voters approved $40,000 in May 2022 and later
  $100,000, the amount today. *(Bill analysis)*
- Under current law, part of what school districts lose to this tax break is covered by the state. *(Fiscal note)*
- The Texas Constitution sets this amount, so an increase also needs voters to approve a change to the
  constitution. *(Sec. 3.02)*

**Why now** *(Analysis)*
The Senate approved the final version 31 to 0 on May 29, 2025, after the House approved it 143 to 0. The Governor
signed it on June 16, 2025; the larger tax break still depends on voters.

**Status** *(Official: the timeline and recorded votes, from the Texas page another builder owns)*

**Who it affects** *(Analysis)*

- Adults in Texas who own the home they live in: $40,000 more of the home's value would be free from school
  district taxes. *(Sec. 1.01)*
- School districts, which would collect less in local property tax and receive state money in its place.
  *(Sec. 1.03)*

**Cost** *(Official)*
Legislative Budget Board fiscal note, May 21, 2025: "a negative impact of ($2,743,461,146) through the biennium
ending August 31, 2027." (A biennium is the state's two-year budget period; an amount in parentheses is a cost.)

**What changes for you** *(Analysis)*
A couple who own and live in a house in Texas would have $140,000 of its value left out of their school tax bill,
up from $100,000. The bill's author estimates this saves a homeowner $363.44 at the statewide average school tax
rate.

**The case for** *(Analysis)*

- The increase would save a homeowner an estimated $363.44 in school taxes at the statewide average rate, and the
  state would make up any funding school districts lose. *(the author's statement of intent in the Senate Research
  Center bill analysis)*

**The case against**
The official analysis that prints arguments on this side could not be read when this summary was written.
*(When an analysis was read and prints nothing against the bill, or none was published, this line reads instead:
"No official record of arguments against this bill was found.")*

**What we don't know** *(Analysis)*

- The sources gathered do not show whether voters approved the change to the Texas Constitution.
- How much the state would owe districts for lost debt payments. The Legislative Budget Board says it cannot be
  determined.

**Do you support raising the school tax break on a home from $100,000 to $140,000?**

Confidence: medium. The bill text, the analysis and the cost estimate are complete, but the tax change depends on a
vote of the people that these sources do not report.

## What I would like you to judge

1. Is "what would change" clear for a bill that is already signed but waits on a public vote ("If this passes")?
2. The cost line uses the Legislative Budget Board's own figure, to the dollar, because the check looks every figure
   up in the sources. Is "$1,324.2 million" acceptable, or do you want rounded figures ("about $1.3 billion") allowed?
3. Is the credit line for an argument ("the author's statement of intent in the Senate Research Center bill
   analysis") the wording you want readers to see?
4. Two different lines can stand where a case against would be: "No official record of arguments against this bill
   was found" (nothing was published) and "The official analysis that prints arguments on this side could not be
   read when this summary was written" (it exists, the site could not open it, and the summary is held for you).
