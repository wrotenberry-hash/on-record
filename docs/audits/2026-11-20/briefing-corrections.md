## 1. H.R. 8483 (Barona Band land)

**Headline**
- Was: Lets the Barona Band add 847 federal acres to its reservation.
- Now: **Adds about 847 federal acres to the reservation of a California tribe, the Barona Band.**

**In plain English** (80 words)
Today, federal agencies manage about 847 acres in San Diego County: 164 acres under the Bureau of Land Management and
683 acres under the Forest Service. This bill would have the United States hold that land in trust for the benefit of a
California tribe, the Barona Band, and make it part of the Tribe's reservation, while keeping existing public access,
water rights, and emergency access, and adding a gambling ban. The House passed it 398–0 on November 17, 2026.

**How it works today**
1. The Barona Band, a tribe, lives on a 7,500-acre reservation in San Diego County, California. Its current reservation is not its original reservation.
2. In 1931, the City of San Diego received congressional approval to buy part of the Capitan Grande Reservation for the El Capitan Reservoir. The committee report says Barona members had mainly lived there and buried ancestors there.
3. The land in this bill totals about 847 acres between the Barona Reservation and the Capitan Grande Reservation. One 164-acre parcel is managed by the Bureau of Land Management. *(unchanged)*
4. Two other parcels, totaling about 683 acres, are managed by the Forest Service. The bill would move those parcels to the Interior Department and place all three parcels in trust for the Tribe.
5. Federal Indian gaming law treats reservation land, and land held in trust by the United States for a tribe over which a tribe exercises governmental power, as Indian lands. This bill separately bans class II gambling, such as bingo-style games, and class III gambling, as that law defines those classes, on these parcels.

**What would change**

| | Today | If this passes |
|---|---|---|
| The 847 acres | Federal agencies manage about 847 acres in San Diego County: one 164-acre Bureau of Land Management parcel and two Forest Service parcels totaling about 683 acres. | The United States must hold all about 847 acres in trust for the benefit of a California tribe, the Barona Band, and the land becomes part of the Tribe's reservation. |
| The 683 Forest Service acres | The Forest Service manages two of the bill's three parcels, Parcels 2 and 3, totaling about 683 acres, including a fuel break. | Those 683 acres must move to the Interior Department and be held in trust for the benefit of a California tribe, the Barona Band. |
| Public use of roads and trails | The public may use roads and trails on these lands under rules in effect on the date the bill becomes law. | The public keeps access for recreation, scenery, science, and conservation, including motorized access to existing roads and recreational access to existing trails, under rules in effect on the date the bill becomes law. |
| Existing rights and agreements | Valid existing liens, rights-of-way and road rights-of-way agreements (rights of access to the land), licenses, leases, permits, special use permits, and easements, along with existing water rights and service agreements, apply on the date the bill becomes law. | The land stays subject to those valid existing rights, and the bill does not alter existing water rights or service agreements. The bill does not reduce existing Forest Service administrative access. |
| Fuel break and emergency access | Federal agencies manage the land, which includes a fuel break on the Forest Service parcels. | Bureau of Land Management and Forest Service employees keep the access the Secretary of the Interior, consulting the Secretary of Agriculture, considers necessary for operations and emergencies, including search and rescue, wildfire suppression, evacuations, fuels management, monitoring, and law enforcement. The Tribe must maintain the fuel break within the 683 Forest Service acres in accordance with applicable Forest Service standards. |
| Gambling on the land | The land, about 847 federal acres, is not part of the reservation of a California tribe, the Barona Band. | The land may not be used for class II gambling, such as bingo-style games, or class III gambling, as the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act defines those classes. |

**What was wrong (18 findings in the core; the main ones)**
- "Lets the Barona Band add": the bill does it itself ("the covered land is taken into trust for the benefit of the Tribe"); no source gives the Band a choice.
- "hold that land for the Barona Band", three times: the source says "in trust for the benefit of". "In trust" was dropped.
- "keeping … a gambling ban": the ban is new ("The legislation includes a gaming prohibition").
- "casino-style gambling": the word is in no source.
- "847 federal acres": the source says "approximately 847 acres".
- "before placing all three parcels in trust": no source gives that order.
- The fuel-break row's "today" side listed uses of the land that no source describes as happening today.
- "Fire break": every source says "fuel break".

**Sections outside the core with faults (would be left off until corrected):** what it does (casino-style); who it
affects (members' floor claims given as fact; "emergency workers" in no source); what changes for you (one member's
claim given as fact; an invented hiker); what we don't know (all three lines talk about "the sources"); the voting
question ("transferring … to the Barona Band"). The reasons for the bill are sound and stay.

**Two points for the owner**
- "Class III gambling" is left unexplained: the saved copy of the gaming law stops before its definition, so no plain gloss could be supported.

## 2. H.R. 9600 (Thomas Paine memorial)

**Headline**
- Was: Lets a Thomas Paine memorial be placed near the National Mall.
- Now: **Requires placing the memorial to Thomas Paine, who argued for independence, near the National Mall.**

**In plain English** (79 words)
Congress already allowed a privately funded memorial to Thomas Paine, whose 1776 pamphlet argued for independence.
Today, putting it in Area I, an area near the National Mall reserved for memorials of "preeminent historical and
lasting significance to the United States," needs another law after a federal recommendation; this bill approves that
location and requires the Paine memorial to be placed there. The House passed it 365–36 on November 17, 2026, and the
Senate received it the next day.

**How it works today**
1. Thomas Paine wrote a 1776 pamphlet that argued the colonies should break from Great Britain. The committee report says it helped build support for the American Revolution.
2. Congress allowed the Thomas Paine Memorial Association to create a privately funded memorial in Washington in 2022. *(unchanged)*
3. Washington memorial sites are divided into areas. Area I is reserved for subjects of "preeminent historical and lasting significance to the United States" and is near the National Mall.
4. Current law allows a memorial in Area I only after a federal recommendation and approval by law within 150 calendar days after notice to Congress.
5. The National Capital Memorial Advisory Commission recommended the Thomas Paine memorial for Area I in 2024, but Rep. Raskin said the recommendation had not been sent to Congress.

**What would change**

| | Today | If this passes |
|---|---|---|
| Where the Thomas Paine memorial may go | The memorial has been authorized, but placement in Area I, an area near the National Mall reserved for memorials of "preeminent historical and lasting significance to the United States," needs approval by law. | The memorial's location in Area I, an area near the National Mall reserved for memorials of "preeminent historical and lasting significance to the United States," is approved, and the memorial must be located there. |
| The usual approval process for Area I, near the National Mall | A federal official may recommend Area I, an area for memorials near the National Mall, after advice from the memorial advisory commission, and the recommendation must be approved by law within 150 calendar days after notice. | This bill approves placement in Area I, an area for memorials near the National Mall, despite the requirements current law sets for placing a memorial in that area. |
| Public funding for the memorial | The committee report says the Thomas Paine Memorial Association is authorized to establish a privately funded memorial. | The bill changes the location approval only. It does not add federal money. |

**What was wrong (11 findings in the core; the main ones)**
- "Lets … be placed": the bill says the memorial "shall be located" there; the research service says the bill "requires" it.
- "the major memorial zone", "Area One": no source uses either. The bill and the law say "Area I".
- "national importance that lasts over time": the law says "preeminent historical and lasting significance"; "preeminent" was dropped.
- "Congress must approve it": the law says the recommendation must be "approved by law"; it puts no duty on Congress.
- "so the Paine memorial can move ahead there": no source says this.
- "in the text provided": wording about the materials, not the bill.

**Sections outside the core with faults:** the reasons for it (one reason credits Rep. Stansbury's words to Rep. Raskin;
another changes what Rep. Westerman said); who it affects (effects on visitors and officials that no source states);
what changes for you (an invented visitor); what we don't know ("The sources do not give …").

**Two points for the owner**
- The headline could read "who urged American independence" (clearer), but "urged" is not a source's word; "argued for independence" is.
- "Near the National Mall" rests on the research service ("Area 1 of the National Mall") and two members' floor statements; the bill itself points only to a map.

## 3. H.Con.Res. 93 (hostilities against Iran)

This one is not on show: it was written on 20 Nov and hidden after its first reading. The Senate rejected it on 19 Nov.

**Headline**
- Was: Requires the President to pull United States troops from fighting Iran.
- Now: **Directs the President to remove United States Armed Forces from hostilities against Iran.**

**In plain English** (79 words)
This resolution would direct the President to remove United States armed forces from hostilities against Iran, unless
Congress declares war or specifically authorizes force against Iran. It would not cover forces needed to defend the
United States, an ally, or a partner from an imminent attack, provided that the President complies fully with section
5(b) of the War Powers Resolution. The House passed it 220–204 on September 15, 2026, but the Senate rejected it 48–51
on November 19, 2026.

**How it works today**
1. The War Powers Resolution is a law about when Presidents must report and end military involvement that Congress has not authorized. *(unchanged)*
2. That law generally requires the President to remove United States armed forces from hostilities within 60 days after the President notifies or is required to notify Congress, unless Congress authorizes the use.
3. This resolution concerns United States armed forces in hostilities against Iran, formally called the Islamic Republic of Iran. *(unchanged)*
4. The resolution would not cover forces needed to defend the United States, an ally, or a partner from imminent attack, provided that the President complies fully with section 5(b) of the War Powers Resolution (the 60-day rule).
5. The House passed the resolution 220–204 on September 15, 2026. The Senate rejected it 48–51 on November 19, 2026. *(unchanged)*

**What would change**

| | Today | If this passes |
|---|---|---|
| United States armed forces in hostilities against Iran | The War Powers Resolution generally requires removal of United States armed forces from hostilities within 60 days after the President notifies or must notify Congress, unless Congress authorizes the use. | Congress would direct the President to remove United States armed forces from hostilities against Iran, unless Congress declares war or specifically authorizes force against Iran. |
| Forces needed to defend from imminent attack | The War Powers Resolution's 60-day limit generally applies unless Congress authorizes the use of force. | Forces could remain if needed to defend the United States, an ally, or a partner from imminent attack, provided that the President complies fully with section 5(b) of the War Powers Resolution (its 60-day limit). |
| Status of the resolution | The House passed it 220–204 on September 15, 2026, and the Senate rejected it 48–51 on November 19, 2026. | Congress would have directed the President to remove United States armed forces from hostilities against Iran, but the resolution did not pass the Senate. |

**Voting question:** Do you support directing the President to remove United States Armed Forces from hostilities against Iran?

**What was wrong**
- "Requires": the text and the research service say "directs"; no source says it binds.
- "troops", "fighting Iran": the text says "United States Armed Forces" and "hostilities against" Iran.
- The defence exception left off its condition ("provided that the President complies fully with … section 5(b)"), in three places.
- The 60-day rule left off "or is required to notify".
- A row said "No source here states that Congress has declared war …": wording about the sources. **That row is removed**: what it said about the bill is already in the first row. (The alternative was to quote Rep. Meeks, a supporter, in a "today" line; a supporter's claim does not belong there.)
- "It would have expressed Congress's direction": "expressed" is in no source.

**Sections outside the core with faults:** what it does; who it affects; what changes for you (predicts a deployment
ending, which nothing in the record says); what we don't know (three lines beginning "The sources do not …"); why now
("the Iran war"; unnamed "supporters" where the record has Rep. Meeks). The reasons for and against are sound: all four
quotes are word for word in the named member's own remarks.

**One point for the owner**
- The text says the resolution acts under section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution; nothing in the sources explains what that section does, so the summary does not mention it.
